# 12. Project walkthrough: how to explain this project in an interview

Written for: **you**, rehearsing how to present the project to a recruiter or interviewer. Practise these out loud; adapt the wording to your own voice.

---

## The 30-second version (opening line)

> "I built a retail sales and inventory analytics system on PostgreSQL. I generated three years of realistic data, wrote about 140 SQL queries and analytical views to answer management questions on sales, customers,
> inventory and returns, checked everything with automated tests, and added Python analysis and a Power BI dashboard specification."

## The 2-minute answer (about 280 words)

> "This project is a small version of what an analytics team does for a retailer. The company is fictional, with eleven stores in India plus an online hub, and management wants to know how the business is doing,
> what is selling, who the good customers are, and what stock is short or piling up.
>
> I started with the database: nineteen normalised tables with keys and constraints. I couldn't find real retail data with returns and stock ledgers, so I wrote a seeded Python simulation of the business itself:
> demand with festive seasonality, stock that falls with sales, reorders that arrive after supplier lead times, returns and payments. That gave me about fifty-seven thousand orders over three years, and I planted known data-quality problems like duplicate customers and a stock ledger that does not reconcile.
>
> Then the SQL: about a hundred and forty queries with window functions, CTEs, recursive CTEs and cohort and RFM analysis, plus twelve reusable views that define every KPI once, and a thirty-check data-quality scorecard.
> I added indexes only where an `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` measurement justified them; for example an index made a ledger lookup hundreds of times faster, while rewriting one slow query beat adding an index at all.
>
> Python reads those views for analysis and charts, and the dashboard is specified for Power BI with a star schema and fifty-five measures. More than three hundred tests reconcile the same numbers across SQL, Python and the expected dashboard values.
>
> What I'm most proud of is the discipline: I separate facts from opinions, state my assumptions, and I'm upfront that the data is synthetic."

**Delivery tips:** slow down at "planted problems", "measured", and "synthetic"; those are the sentences that build trust.

## Follow-up questions you should expect (and honest answers)

**"Is this real data?"** No, and I say so first. It is synthetic, generated by a simulation of the business process. Patterns such as seasonality were designed in, so finding them shows the analysis works, not that real customers behave this way.

**"Why is your repeat rate so high?"** It is a property of my generator (83 %). Real retail would be lower. I did not tune it further because the point is the method, and I note it in the documentation.

**"Why is dead stock more than half your inventory value?"** Two reasons: the store-product demand is sparse (many positions sell a few units a year), and my rule is "no sale at that store in 365 days". With 180 days it would be 63 %. The threshold is an assumption and I show its sensitivity.

**"Did you build the Power BI report?"** State exactly where you are: either "I specified it completely, with a star schema, 55 measures, theme, page specs and expected values, and I validated the specification with automated tests" or, if you built it, "I built it and reconciled the cards to the expected values" and show screenshots. Do not claim what you have not done.

**"What would you do differently in production?"** Incremental loads, a proper customer-matching process, a safety-stock model and forecasting, monitoring of data-quality checks, row-level security and scheduled refresh through a gateway.

**"What was the biggest mistake?"** Pick one you can tell well: (a) I first created several indexes without measuring; testing showed some were ignored or useless, so I removed them; (b) my benchmark ran inside a transaction that rolled back, so the indexes never persisted, which I found by inspecting the database; (c) a sentence in my own write-up said October was the best month every year, but 2023's best was November, which a check caught.

## Things to volunteer (they build credibility)

- The data is synthetic and how it was made.
- Definitions come first: revenue is net of GST; AOV is GST-inclusive; sales are completed orders only.
- Thresholds (RFM, stock health, ABC) are my assumptions.
- A benchmark gap with an identical plan is noise, not speed-up.
- Before/after promotion numbers do not prove cause.

## Things not to claim

- Real-world business results or "increased revenue" of any company.
- A production system, real users, or a published Power BI report (unless you did it).
- Forecasting, machine learning or safety-stock modelling (not implemented).
- Speed-ups you have not measured, or precise benchmark numbers without saying "on my laptop, warm cache, repeated runs".
